# Contextual equivalence across languages and resources

Use when a term, concept or definition must be aligned across languages, domains or historical periods. Do not equate words simply because a dictionary lists one as a translation.

1. Establish source and candidate concept/sense with definition, actual usages, domain, locale/register, date and applicable authority. Compare concept properties, scope, referents, pragmatic implications and professional conventions. Keep the source term and exact evidence recoverable. Use translation of exact necessary passages or analysis of actual paired versions only when material; don't claim reconstructed speech or attestation without evidence.
2. Characterize relation in the intended context: exact for the scoped sense, approximate/overlapping, broader, narrower, no satisfactory direct equivalent found, or unresolved. These are descriptive judgments, not numerical confidence. A close match A–B and B–C does not establish A–C; check relevant endpoints. Relations may be directional and scoped; avoid asserting universal equivalence from one resource.
3. Compare candidate labels and expression options: existing term, qualified compound, borrowed/retained original where the audience convention warrants, descriptive paraphrase or annotation. Preserve meaning/authority and note trade-offs; the absence of a single word does not make a concept untranslatable. Explicitly distinguish term form, concept overlap and normative/project preference. Do not impose a translated form when domain practice legitimately uses an English term.
4. Return a grounded scoped mapping, evidence, alternatives and limitations. Where multiple versions are coequal or legal/domain authority is disputed, report divergence; do not choose the authoritative meaning on behalf of the responsible owner. Do not auto-approve or propagate new labels across documents without authority.

Synthetic example: a product's English `session` may be an authentication lifetime or a workshop event. Ukrainian counterparts depend on actual sense/context; neither a glossary label nor matching spelling proves the mapping. Test the relevant concept and use case before choosing.
