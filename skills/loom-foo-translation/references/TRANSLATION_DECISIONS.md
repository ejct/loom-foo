# Translation decisions

Use for meaningful ambiguity, voice or competing strategies. Start with the user's purpose; do not silently expand it.

## Meaning and context

Read the whole supplied context before choosing local word senses. Track explicit assertions, presuppositions, plausible implicatures, referents, scope, modality, time/aspect, evidentiality, social relations and register separately. An inference supported by context is not necessarily stated in the source. Distinguish genuine polysemy/ambiguity from vagueness and lack of context. Never infer identity, gender, intent or a historical fact simply because target grammar invites a choice.

For a consequential ambiguous span: identify candidate readings; check context and genre; test a natural target phrasing preserving them; if not feasible, choose a well-supported reading, a neutral paraphrase or an explicitly labeled alternative. Ask only if the choice materially blocks the deliverable. Preserve ambiguity deliberately used for humor, suspense or diplomacy when possible. An added footnote may preserve a distinction for a reader but does not make the target sentence itself equivalent in form.

## Strategy trade-offs

- Natural: produce idiomatic target prose without replacing the author's distinctive voice with generic fluent prose.
- Literal: preserve close structure for study, but mark where word-for-word mapping would mislead. Provide a gloss separately if useful.
- Semantic: prioritize distinctions, scope and referents even if a longer rendering is needed.
- Literary: consider imagery, rhythm, sound, repetition, irony, genre and voice. Rank constraints from the brief; present alternatives if preserving one sacrifices another. Do not invent a rhyme by changing the claim without disclosure.
- Pragmatic: preserve communicative function where supported; distinguish literal content from inferred intent. Do not universalize culture or register.
- Adaptive: adjust idioms, examples or conventions only within explicit purpose; label consequential substitution, omission or reframing.
- Historical: use the historical evidence method, not generic archaizing.
- Annotated: deliver the translation plus selective notes on material choices, not a commentary on every token.

Example: EN “You were right.” → UK “Ви мали рацію.” The target selects formal singular or plural, excluding informal singular; it does not establish which of the remaining readings is intended. “Ти мав/мала рацію” are contextual alternatives, not universally preferable. Do not claim English lacks ways to express formality; the source pronoun alone does not encode that distinction.

Example: EN “Could you open the window?” can be a request rather than a question about ability. Preserve the likely function without claiming it is the only possible reading. A medical dosage, contract condition or quoted promise requires especially careful scope and exactness.

## Mediated communication (textual scope)

Where the material includes an interpreted or mediated exchange, keep speaker identity, original utterance, its rendering, and the mediator’s own clarification/correction or turn-management intervention distinct. Preserve speech act, stance and attribution; never insert an explanation into a speaker’s words as if said by that speaker. Mark missing utterances/uncertain attribution rather than inventing them. E.g. praise rendered as advice changes communicative function even if the topic is preserved. Label the mediator’s clarification separately. Do not infer actual audio/prosody or claim live turn management, signed interpretation, lip sync or professional qualification from text alone.

## Verification

Compare all assertions, protected details and discourse links. Check pronoun antecedents, idioms, numbers, negation, conditionals, quoted speaker and emotional intensity. Compare competing formulations when a material decision is unresolved. Check target naturalness separately from fidelity. If external evidence is unavailable, mark the limitation; never invent a dictionary entry. Do not request unnecessary research for ordinary low-risk phrases.
