# Translatability and counterexamples

When asked what cannot be translated, specify the property, context and constraints before concluding that a direct equivalent is absent. Distinguish lexeme, phrase, discourse effect and cultural experience. A language may convey a meaning using more words, a metaphor, a gloss, or contextual setup; lack of a one-word equivalent is not absolute untranslatability.

Test progressively: direct natural correspondence; alternate construction; contextual recovery; descriptive paraphrase; annotation; competing priorities (e.g. sound versus content). Show a concrete alternative for any consequential claim of unavoidable loss, or mark that no satisfactory alternative was found with current evidence. Compare alternative against all relevant properties rather than privileging literal matching.

Classify feasible preservation as `direct`, `contextual`, `descriptive`, `annotated`, `tradeoff` or `unresolved`. These are option classes, not universal ordinal scores. State the trade-off: length, readability, ambiguity, naturalness, register, form, reader effort, or source fidelity. A note can restore knowledge for the reader, not the in-text experience. If the user needs strict one-line copy or a poem of fixed meter, report that constraint explicitly.

Do not use stereotyped “untranslatable words” lists or unsourced claims that an entire culture lacks a concept. Context, language variation and individual use matter. For obscure linguistic assertions, check actual lexical/corpus evidence where available. An alternative that fails to preserve another important property is evidence of a trade-off, not a successful full replacement.
