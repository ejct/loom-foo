# Transformation method

## Compare properties, not just words

First establish the text versions and relevant context. Form a falsifiable property claim: e.g., source explicitly asserts P; target permits P but does not assert it. Align one or more exact source and target anchors; check nearby and distributed context. Distinguish what a text entails, presupposes, conventionally suggests, conversationally implicates, or merely allows as a possibility. Do not infer what a reader actually believed without evidence.

For each relevant property: determine source state and target state (explicit, implicit, possible, absent, unknown); classify the directional change; describe reader consequence; assess relative to brief. When the target language adds an obligatory grammatical distinction, test neutral recasting before claiming necessity. A shift in surface form can preserve meaning entirely. In reverse, target text may use the same lexeme but a changed sense. No finding is acceptable when no material change is supported.

## Type definitions

- `preserved`: the property remains available at comparable strength for the intended context.
- `lost`: a source-licensed distinction or effect is no longer available from the target in the agreed context. If recoverable only via an annotation or the original, distinguish channels.
- `added`: new claim, presupposition, implication, signal or distinction not licensed by the source/context; qualify whether it is asserted or merely suggested.
- `shifted`: changed meaning, strength, focus, register, speaker relation or effect that is not best described as a simple set inclusion.
- `narrowed`: target excludes one or more materially viable source readings.
- `broadened`: target permits additional materially viable readings. Not every ambiguity is a defect.
- `explicitated`: previously inferable content is made overt without necessarily adding a new fact.
- `implicitated`: formerly explicit content becomes inferable rather than stated. Do not label total loss if recoverable in context.

These are property-level descriptions, not mutually exclusive text-level grades. `unknown` is a state of knowledge, not a proven change. When a narrowing adds a specific claim, distinguish the two affected properties; do not report the same issue twice just to increase coverage.

## Dimensions and assessment

Check semantic, logical, pragmatic, cultural, social, temporal, emotional, stylistic, aesthetic and structural dimensions only where relevant. Examples: actor and action; negation and quantifier scope; indirect request; honorifics; historical word sense; degree of certainty; irony; sound and wordplay; narrative and markup. Structural changes need no adverse finding if they preserve the requested result.

Assess each finding against the actual brief: `structurally_conditioned`, `contextually_justified`, `stylistic_tradeoff`, `error`, `unresolved`, `not_applicable`. More than one condition may apply; select the dominant assessment and explain nuance. Structural conditioning does not automatically excuse a bad translation. Optional MQM error labels can be attached for actual errors, not all changes; refer to https://www.themqm.org/mqm-pillars/typology/. Distinguish severity/reader impact from confidence in the observation. If the brief is missing, evaluate factual distortion but keep optional style judgments conditional.

## Mediated speech and attribution

When comparing a mediated exchange, anchor who said what, who rendered it, and any distinct interpreter clarification. Compare speech act, stance, obligation and audience relationship; separate observed speaker/recipient responses from inferred possible effect. Praise translated as a recommendation may create apparent agreement about different things; do not assert actual misunderstanding or mediator motive without evidence. Preserve transcript/source coverage and don't infer audio/prosody or live performance from text.

## Evidence check

For each material finding: cite actual anchors; verify context recovery; test one plausible alternative when consequential; state what evidence would change the assessment. If a claim relies on historical usage, register or culture, consult appropriate sources or qualify it. Never invent grammar rules, word etymologies, translator motivations or exact probabilities. Use target-only observation only when source is unavailable and label it as such. If a claim is uncertain, keep it separate from confirmed loss.

Example EN “You were right.” → UK “Ти мав рацію.” The informal singular masculine grammatical choice narrows source possibilities. It need not imply biological sex; context might license the choice. Without context this is a supported distinction with an unresolved quality assessment, not proof of translator error. “Ви мали рацію” selects a different range of readings; neither necessarily preserves every distinction.

Example EN “She did not promise to pay.” → UK “Вона пообіцяла не платити.” Scope of negation moves from promise to payment; materially different assertion. This is an accuracy error under a faithful brief, even if words overlap.

Example EN “I have a red car.” → UK “У мене червона машина.” Different grammatical possession structure; the main assertion survives. Do not claim loss of the verb “have” as loss of possession.
