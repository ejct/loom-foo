# Match information shape to visual form

Choose a visual grammar for the relationship actually supported by the source:

| Task | Candidate form | Key trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compare ranked quantities | Sorted bars, dot plot, aligned small multiples | Inconsistent baselines, area implying extra magnitude |
| Show change over time | Line chart, aligned series, timeline for events | Irregular time treated as equal intervals; trend mistaken for causality |
| Part-to-whole | Stacked display when total and basis meaningful | Percentages with shifting denominators; pie slices too small |
| Distribution | Histogram, density/interval where sample adequate | Bin choices hiding outliers; ungrounded smoothness |
| Process/sequence | Directed flow with explicit decisions and terminal states | Missing exceptions, feedback loops, false linearity |
| Hierarchy | Indented tree or nested groups | Fake one-to-one taxonomy from ambiguous categories |
| Network or dependency | Nodes+typed edges with arrow semantics | Unreadable spaghetti, arrow direction implied but unsupported |
| Spatial relation | Grounded map/scale/georeferenced diagram | Placements or distances invented by visual layout |
| Concepts/technical mechanism | Labeled explanatory diagram | Metaphorical arrows misread as measured physical processes |

## Legibility strategy

State the one or two relationships readers must take away, then minimize competing decorative elements. Prefer direct labels over legend decoding when possible; keep full units and time references. Show exceptions, missing values, uncertainty or confidence bands when they materially affect interpretation. For dense figures, progressive disclosure or separate panels may be more honest than shrinking every label.

A visual topology is a design decision; changing geometry does not authorize changing factual source relationships.
